# Stream 2: EaaS, Dealer Channel and Demand Response Norms

Research stream covering Group 1 companies 11 to 20 from Partner-Comp-Research-Plan.md: Redaptive, Sparkfund, Metrus Energy, Budderfly, Sealed, Carrier / Lennox / Daikin dealer programs, Enphase Installer Network, SunPower / Sunrun dealer and affiliate programs, Generac PowerPro, and the demand response group (Voltus, Enel X, CPower, Leap).

Compiled 2026-09-27. Every rate below carries a source and date. Where a company does not publish partner economics, that is stated explicitly and what could be inferred is labeled as an estimate or inference, not a fact.

## 1. Redaptive

**Who the partner is.** Redaptive is an Efficiency as a Service (EaaS) provider, not itself a channel-partner-driven seller in the way a dealer network is. Its "partner ecosystem" (redaptive.com/partners/, fetched 2026-09-27) groups partners into three types: Ecosystem Partners (REITs, developers, data analytics firms, consultants doing co-branded solution development), Technology and Service Providers (EPC firms, electrical and mechanical contractors, system integrators, O&M providers, equipment OEMs, audit companies who execute projects), and Studio Partners (early-stage tech companies for pilots).

**Basis of pay.** Redaptive's core revenue model with the end customer is a shared-savings/pay-for-performance structure: it invoices the customer monthly based on actual kWh saved during the contract term (redaptive.com/what-is-redaptives-efficiency-as-a-service-eaas-model/, fetched 2026-09-27), delivering 5 percent to 30 percent immediate bill savings versus the prior lighting, HVAC or water bill. It funds 100 percent of project capital.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** No public split of the savings percentage that Redaptive keeps versus what the customer keeps. No public referral fee or commission percentage for EPC, contractor or ecosystem partners. Source: redaptive.com/partners/, fetched 2026-09-27, "contains no explicit mention of referral fees, commission structures, revenue sharing percentages, or compensation mechanisms." This is a gap, not a finding, and is marked as not public.

**What is a fee vs a share.** Inferred structure only (not confirmed by Redaptive): EPCs and contractors appear to be paid as a project cost (a fee, going rate for installation work) while Redaptive itself takes the shared-savings margin as the technology/capital owner. This is an inference from how EaaS deals are typically structured industry-wide, not a Redaptive-specific disclosure.

**Stacking rules.** Not public.

**Attribution.** Not public. Redaptive's own PMO and structured-finance function suggests Redaptive, not the partner, generally owns the client relationship and deal economics; partners are described as delivering "scalable deal flow" and "pre-qualified project scopes" to Redaptive rather than the reverse.

**Duration and tail.** The EaaS contract itself runs multi-year (industry-typical 5 to 15 years for EaaS/ESA structures); at contract end, the customer owns the installed assets and keeps 100 percent of savings going forward. No public data on whether a partner who introduced the deal keeps earning on repeat/phase-two work.

**Payment timing and holdbacks.** Not public for partners. Customer is billed monthly against metered savings.

**Clawbacks.** Not public.

**Who bears performance risk.** Redaptive bears savings-shortfall risk with the customer under the shared-savings/EaaS model (this is the point of the model), not the contractor or EPC partner, who is paid as a delivery vendor.

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay.** Not public.

**Transparency.** Partners are directed to "Contact Us" for partnership terms; no published rate card. Low transparency to prospective partners before engagement.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** No lawsuits, Glassdoor or Reddit threads specific to Redaptive's partner economics turned up in this research pass. Redaptive is well established (AT&T Efficiency-as-a-Service Program is a marquee case study, Better Buildings Solution Center, fetched 2026-09-27) but that describes the customer program, not partner pay.

**Verdict.** Redaptive is a platform-and-capital company that treats delivery partners as paid vendors and keeps the shared-savings margin itself; it does not appear to run a public channel/referral commission program at all, which is itself informative for Energy Plus: at least one large, well-funded EaaS competitor pays for installation work as a fee and does not share its financial-owner margin with intermediaries.

## 3. Metrus Energy

**Who the partner is.** Metrus is an EaaS/shared-savings financier. It contracts directly with the end customer through a Sustainable Energy Services Agreement (SESA) and pays a third-party contractor to engineer, install and maintain the project. In the flagship case documented by DOE's Better Buildings Solution Center (betterbuildingssolutioncenter.energy.gov/implementation-models/metrus-energy-nationwide-deployment-efficiency-services-agreement, fetched 2026-09-27), Siemens was the contractor Metrus paid to implement the project; Metrus was later acquired by Engie subsidiary ECP (metrusenergy.com/resources/blog/metrus-acquisition-by-ecp, fetched 2026-09-27).

**Basis of pay.** Per DOE's description, ESA payments are based on energy units saved (avoided kWh, "negawatts"), at a fixed, output-based price per unit of savings set at or below the customer's existing utility rate, so the customer's savings begin on day one and Metrus is paid on a per-unit-of-avoided-energy basis, not a percentage-of-savings basis. Source: betterbuildingssolutioncenter.energy.gov, fetched 2026-09-27.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** No public per-kWh price or percentage split. Key Equipment Finance was Metrus's financing partner on multiple EaaS deals (key.com/businesses-institutions/business-expertise/articles/client-success-metrus-energy.html, fetched 2026-09-27); this is financing, not partner commission.

**What is a fee vs a share.** The contractor (for example Siemens in the DOE case study) is paid a project fee for engineering, installation and maintenance. Metrus's own return comes from the spread between the fixed avoided-cost price it charges the customer and its actual cost of capital plus maintenance, i.e., Metrus takes the savings-share risk, the contractor takes a fee.

**Stacking, attribution, duration and tail, payment timing, clawbacks.** Not public. SESA contracts are typically 10 to 15 years industry-wide (general ESA/EaaS norm, not Metrus-specific), during which time Metrus, not a channel partner, holds the customer relationship.

**Who bears performance risk.** Metrus, as the ESA provider, bears the shortfall risk on savings performance; this is the entire premise of shared-savings/ESA financing (this is confirmed for the ESA/EaaS category generally by DOE and NAESCO material referenced across this research, not specific to a leaked Metrus contract).

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay, transparency.** Not public.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** No lawsuits or Reddit/Glassdoor threads found. Metrus is a small, largely B2B-invisible company; the main public trace is its DOE case studies and its 2022 to 2023 area acquisition by ECP.

**Verdict.** Metrus is the cleanest confirmation in this whole research stream of the "fee for work, share for capital/performance risk" split Energy Plus is already building toward in Model B: the delivery contractor gets paid a fee, the capital and performance-risk holder (Metrus) keeps the shared-savings spread.

## 4. Budderfly

**Who the partner is.** Budderfly is a direct-to-customer EaaS company for restaurants, retail and fitness (it owns the equipment, "no partner" needed to close in the traditional sense), but it also runs co-branded corporate partnerships with multi-unit restaurant groups (Craveworthy Brands, Dunkin' Profitability Subcommittee, O'Charley's/99 Restaurants) that function like enterprise-level channel deals bringing Budderfly access to franchise systems. Sources: news.budderfly.com/budderfly-and-craveworthy-brands, businesswire.com/news/home/20221208005076 (O'Charley's/99 Restaurants), both fetched 2026-09-27.

**Basis of pay to the customer (the closest public analog to a savings-share formula).** This is the single most useful primary-quality data point in this stream. Budderfly's CEO Al Subbloie described the exact savings waterfall to Canary Media (canarymedia.com/articles/energy-efficiency/a-novel-way-to-improve-energy-efficiency-take-over-the-utility-bill, published 2024-01-23, fetched 2026-09-27):
- Customer gets an immediate 5 percent discount off the prior utility bill, from day one.
- The next roughly 15 percent of savings pays back Budderfly's own capital and install cost.
- Budderfly earns its revenue/margin on savings from there up to about 30 percent total.
- Any savings beyond 30 percent are split with the customer.
Average realized savings run 30 percent to 35 percent (same source).

**What is a fee vs a share.** This is entirely a share structure (percent of avoided spend), tiered by waterfall position, not a fee-for-work model. There is no public "installer fee" line because Budderfly self-performs or subcontracts installation as a cost inside its own capital stack, not as a separate partner payment.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** As above, Canary Media, 2024-01-23. No update found changing these percentages as of 2026-09-27.

**Stacking rules, attribution.** Not applicable in the traditional multi-partner sense; Budderfly owns the customer relationship for the life of the utility-bill takeover.

**Duration and tail.** Budderfly's model is a long-term "utility of record" takeover, not a fixed-term project; the relationship (and the waterfall) persists as long as Budderfly is billing the site, which functions as a very long tail with no separate phase-two payment question, because it is one continuous engagement.

**Payment timing and holdbacks.** Budderfly bills monthly in place of the utility. Not applicable to partner holdbacks since there is no third-party channel commission disclosed.

**Clawbacks.** None disclosed to a channel partner (there is no disclosed partner clawback because there is no disclosed partner commission). On the customer side, the guarantee runs the other way: "if the upgrades don't lower energy use, Budderfly eats it and they don't get any money" (Canary Media, 2024-01-23), i.e., Budderfly self-penalizes on underperformance rather than clawing back from a partner.

**Who bears performance risk.** Budderfly, entirely. This is the headline of its pitch.

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay.** Not public. The corporate multi-brand partnerships (Craveworthy, Dunkin', O'Charley's) look like they function as endorsement/access deals at the franchisor level rather than paid referral arrangements, but no compensation terms are public for those relationships.

**Transparency.** High to the end customer on the waterfall logic (Budderfly's CEO discusses the 5/15/30 percent breakpoints openly in press), but there is no public channel-partner rate card because Budderfly does not appear to run a traditional paid-referral channel.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** Budderfly raised 550 million dollars in March 2026 to scale (pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/03/26/budderfly-raises-550-million..., fetched 2026-09-27) and reported revenue growth to about 110 million dollars in 2023, roughly doubling in two years (Canary Media, 2024-01-23). No lawsuits or Reddit/Glassdoor threads on commission disputes found, consistent with Budderfly not running a commissioned sales channel in the way solar or HVAC dealers do.

**Verdict.** Budderfly is the strongest evidence in this entire stream for a tiered, waterfall-based savings share (fixed discount first, capital payback second, margin band third, upside split last) as an alternative or supplement to Energy Plus's flat percentage-of-Deal-Profit approach. It is a genuinely different design worth flagging for the synthesis: pay back capital and guarantee a floor discount before anyone (including Energy Plus) takes a percentage cut.

## 5. Sealed

**Who the partner is.** Sealed is a residential EaaS/financing company that only works through BPI-certified home performance contractors as its delivery partner network; homeowners never pay Sealed directly for equipment, Sealed pays for the upgrade and is repaid from savings.

**Basis of pay to the homeowner/customer.** Sealed charges the homeowner a fixed price per unit of saved energy that escalates 3 percent per year (general description consistent across sources; not independently verified against a live customer contract in this pass). Sealed also offers "HomeAdvance," described as a "pay with your savings" option where the customer pays only what they actually save, with Sealed guaranteeing that performance to the lending bank. Source: search-aggregated description from sealed.com and third-party coverage, fetched 2026-09-27; treat the 3 percent escalator figure as reported, not independently re-verified on Sealed's own contract page, which could not be fully extracted in this pass.

**Basis of pay to the contractor.** Not confirmed in detail. One search snippet (pre-fetch aggregation, fetched 2026-09-27) states "Sealed payments are completed at time of install so you don't have to wait for or chase program payments," which suggests contractors are paid a fixed amount at time of installation (a fee, not a savings share) rather than waiting on the ongoing homeowner savings stream. This could not be confirmed against Sealed's own site text (sealed.com/faq was fetched directly and did not contain contractor-payment details), so it is marked as an inference from indirect source material, not a verified fact.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** No public contractor fee percentage or dollar figure found.

**What is a fee vs a share.** Based on the available (unverified) evidence: contractor is paid a fee at install; Sealed itself takes the ongoing savings-share risk and reward from the homeowner relationship, similar in shape to Metrus and Redaptive.

**Stacking, attribution.** Not public.

**Duration and tail.** The homeowner-facing payment obligation runs for a multi-year term tied to the financed measure's useful life (industry-typical for residential EaaS, not independently confirmed for Sealed specifically in this pass).

**Payment timing and holdbacks.** Contractor reportedly paid at install (see caveat above); not confirmed whether any holdback applies pending M&V.

**Clawbacks.** Not public.

**Who bears performance risk.** Sealed, via its guarantee to the financing bank behind HomeAdvance.

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay, transparency.** Not public.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** No lawsuits, Reddit or Glassdoor threads on Sealed contractor economics found in this pass. Sealed is noted as "the first ENERGY STAR Home Upgrade Service Provider" (sealed.com, fetched 2026-09-27), which is a marketing/certification claim, not financial disclosure.

**Verdict.** Sealed is directionally the same pattern as Metrus and Redaptive (contractor gets a fee for work, platform keeps the savings-share risk) but the details are thin and partly inferred; do not cite Sealed's numbers as verified in any Energy Plus external document. This is a case where the plan's instruction to "say so" when terms are not public clearly applies.

## 6. Carrier, Lennox and Daikin dealer programs

These are the closest real analogs to Energy Plus's "diagnostics, installation ... going rate, paid as a project cost" layer, because HVAC OEM dealer programs are built almost entirely around margin, rebates and co-op funds rather than a percentage-of-profit share.

### 6a. Carrier

**Who the partner is.** Independent HVAC dealers, tiered into Factory Authorized Dealer (FAD) status versus standard "Cool Cash participating dealer" status. Source: carrier.com/residential/en/us/why-carrier/become-a-dealer/, carrierincentives.com/promotion/help/faqs.action, both fetched 2026-09-27.

**Basis of pay.** Dealer margin on equipment purchase price (standard manufacturer-to-dealer wholesale margin, not disclosed publicly as a percentage) plus a manufacturer-to-consumer rebate program ("Cool Cash") that drives sell-through of Infinity Series high-efficiency equipment, plus co-op marketing funds for FADs. FAD dealers have two rebate options under Cool Cash (carrierincentives.com, fetched 2026-09-27); the specific dollar values were not extractable from the public FAQ in this pass.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** No public percentage margin figures. Consumer-facing seasonal rebates up to 1,650 dollars per qualifying system were reported in aggregated trade coverage (fetched 2026-09-27); these are consumer rebates, not dealer margin, and are marked as illustrative of program scale, not a partner pay rate.

**What is a fee vs a share.** All fee/margin based. There is no revenue-share or profit-share component anywhere in an OEM HVAC dealer program; the dealer buys at a wholesale price and sells at a marked-up retail price, full stop. This is the cleanest evidence in the entire research plan that in a mature, high-volume trade channel, "pay for work at a going rate" (Model A/B's Layer 1) is the entire model, with no separate "share of profit" layer.

**Stacking rules.** A dealer can stack manufacturer rebates, its own installed-price markup, financing origination fees (many OEM captive or partner lenders pay dealers a fee or reduced rate buy-down for offering financing), and co-op marketing funds simultaneously. This is meaningfully different from Energy Plus's "one closer, no stacking abuse" design goal, and worth flagging: HVAC dealer programs are explicitly designed to stack multiple incentive types on the same sale.

**Attribution.** Not applicable; the dealer is both the seller and the installer, so there is no separate "who gets credit" dispute in the two-party OEM to dealer relationship (disputes instead show up between the dealer's own sales reps, see HVAC spiff data below).

**Duration and tail.** No repeat-business share disclosed; FAD status must be re-earned or maintained annually against training, satisfaction score and volume requirements.

**Payment timing and holdbacks.** Rebates typically paid or credited after the qualifying period closes (seasonal promotion structure); no clawback disclosed beyond standard warranty/fraud provisions.

**Clawbacks.** Not public in the material reviewed.

**Who bears performance risk.** The dealer bears installation-quality and warranty-labor risk; Carrier backs parts warranty and, for FADs, offers a "100 percent Satisfaction Guarantee" that implies some service-quality backstop from Carrier (carrier.com/residential, fetched 2026-09-27).

**Recruiting/sub-partner pay.** None; dealer programs are single-level (dealer buys and sells; no dealer-recruits-dealer override structure found).

**Transparency.** Program tiers (FAD vs Cool Cash participant vs standard) are publicly named, but actual margin percentages are not published; dealers learn real numbers through their distributor relationship, not a public rate card.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** ACHR News trade coverage (achrnews.com/articles/163843, fetched 2026-09-27) frames OEM incentive programs explicitly as a tool to help contractors "cash in on IRA incentives," i.e., stacking manufacturer rebates with the federal Inflation Reduction Act tax credits/rebates is treated as a selling point, further confirming the stacking norm in this channel.

### 6b. Lennox

**Who the partner is.** Three explicit tiers: Participating (entry level, license and insurance only), Allied (added training, CSAT requirements, typically stock parts), and Premier (highest training, CSAT, and sales-volume bar; fewer than 5 percent of Lennox dealers nationwide hold Premier status). Source: lennoxpros.com/partner-resources/premier and /premier-faqs, both fetched 2026-09-27.

**Basis of pay.** Same fee/margin structure as Carrier: wholesale-to-retail markup plus enhanced co-op marketing funds and free training/incentives at the Premier tier. No public percentage figures for margin or co-op fund size.

**Rates and tiers.** Qualitative tier descriptions only, source and date as above; no dollar or percentage figures were publicly disclosed in the pages reviewed.

**Everything else (stacking, attribution, duration, payment timing, clawbacks, risk holder, recruiting pay, transparency).** Structurally identical to Carrier's program: fee/margin only, no share-of-profit layer, no sub-dealer recruiting override, tier status is a gate to co-op funds and lead-generation benefits rather than a different commission math.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** No lawsuits or commission-dispute threads specific to the Lennox dealer tier system found in this pass; general HVAC-industry spiff and commission data (see item 6c/HVAC spiff section under Generac, sourced from pipelineon.com, fetched 2026-09-27) applies across all three OEM brands since it describes contractor-business-level sales compensation, not brand-specific terms.

### 6c. Daikin

**Who the partner is.** Daikin Comfort Pro dealers, part of Daikin's broader marketing accrual fund program.

**Basis of pay.** Daikin's accrual marketing program pays dealers back a percentage of qualifying purchase volume into a marketing fund: up to 8 percent back on Daikin FIT systems generally, with Comfort Pro dealers earning a 2 percent bonus on top, for roughly 10 percent total accrual. Source: aggregated description drawn from irp.cdn-website.com/75bd0a32/files/uploaded/DCP_Loyalty_Program_Brochure.pdf (2024 Daikin Comfort Pro Loyalty Program Brochure) and daikin-tmi.com/marketing-funds, both referenced 2026-09-27. Note: the brochure PDF itself could not be text-extracted in this pass (binary/encoded); the 8 percent and 10 percent figures come from the WebSearch summary of that document plus the daikin-tmi.com marketing-funds page, not a direct read of the brochure text, so treat as reported rather than independently re-verified word for word.

**What is a fee vs a share.** A percentage-of-purchase-volume accrual credited to a marketing fund, which the dealer must spend on approved marketing rather than pocket as cash; this is closer to a rebate/marketing co-op fee than a profit share. 100 percent of accrued funds become available once minimum purchase requirements are met, with no dealer matching contribution required (per the sources above).

**Rates and tiers, stacking, attribution, duration, payment timing, clawbacks, risk holder, recruiting pay, transparency.** Same pattern as Carrier and Lennox: fee/rebate only, tiered by purchase volume and program participation, no profit-share layer, no recruiting override. Funds are described as expiring or tied to a program year (typical for accrual marketing funds industry-wide; not independently confirmed for Daikin's specific expiration terms in this pass).

**Verdict for the equipment/dealer group (Carrier, Lennox, Daikin) as a whole.** This is the strongest, most consistent evidence in the whole research stream that a mature, high-volume, competitive trade channel runs on margin and rebate/co-op fees only, no profit-share layer, no multi-level recruiting override, and heavy stacking of multiple incentive types on the same sale. It validates Energy Plus's Layer 1 ("every task pays a going-rate fee") as industry-normal, and it is a caution against adding a Layer 3 profit share on top of a channel that is used to thinking purely in margin percentage terms. It also validates that stacking rebates plus financing plus co-op funds on one sale is normal in this specific channel, which cuts against a strict "no stacking" design principle if Energy Plus ever sells through an HVAC-dealer-style channel rather than a project-introducer channel.

## 7. Enphase Installer Network (EIN)

**Who the partner is.** Solar installer companies enrolled in Enphase's tiered installer network (Silver, Gold, Platinum). Source: enphase.com/enphase-installer-network-terms, fetched 2026-09-27; enphase.com/installers/resources/ein, fetched 2026-09-27.

**Basis of pay.** This is not a cash-commission program from Enphase to the installer. It is a tiered-benefits program: better placement on the public installer locator (Platinum listed first, then Gold, then Silver), priority product access, branded marketing support, and (per third-party description) storage certification eligibility. Installers make their money from their own retail installation price to the homeowner; Enphase does not appear to pay a per-watt or per-unit commission to network installers based on the sources reviewed.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** Silver, Gold and Platinum, based on undisclosed criteria in a separate "Program Guide" (enphase.com/installers/resources/ein) that is not itself a public rate card; the Terms document (enphase.com/enphase-installer-network-terms, fetched 2026-09-27) explicitly defers all tier criteria and benefit specifics to that guide and states "Participant must comply at all times with all of the Criteria applicable to such Tier," without stating the criteria. Third-party description (WebSearch summary, fetched 2026-09-27) says Platinum requires the longest track record of exclusively installing Enphase products plus top customer-satisfaction/NPS scores and storage certification; Gold has a long track record and storage certification; Silver has installed "a sizeable number" of Enphase systems, with some but not all Silver installers storage-certified. No numeric NPS threshold or installed-unit volume figure is public.

**What is a fee vs a share.** Neither, in the traditional sense; this is a marketing/lead-generation benefits tier, not a payment mechanism at all.

**Stacking rules.** Not applicable.

**Attribution.** Not applicable (Enphase does not split commission between installers).

**Duration and tail.** Either party may terminate the EIN agreement with 10 days' written notice for any reason; Enphase may terminate immediately for failure to meet program requirements (enphase.com/enphase-installer-network-terms, fetched 2026-09-27). Tier status is not a permanent asset and must be maintained.

**Payment timing, holdbacks, clawbacks.** Not applicable; there is no cash payment stream to claw back.

**Who bears performance risk.** The installer bears installation-quality and warranty-labor risk for their own work; Enphase's product warranty covers the hardware itself, separately.

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay.** None found.

**Transparency.** Low on the specifics; tier names and general positioning benefits are public, but the actual qualifying criteria live in a non-public or gated Program Guide.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** No lawsuits or Reddit/Glassdoor threads specific to EIN tier disputes found in this pass.

**Verdict.** Enphase's model is instructive as a contrast, not a template: it rewards volume and quality with visibility and marketing benefits, not cash, cleanly separating "who gets paid for the work" (the installer, at their own retail price) from "who gets rewarded for loyalty and quality" (better placement, not money). There is nothing here analogous to Energy Plus's "bring in a partner" cash override question.

## 8. SunPower and Sunrun dealer and affiliate programs

### 8a. SunPower

**Who the partner is.** Independent solar dealers in a three-tier program: Elite, Premier and Authorized. Source: SunPower 10-K, fiscal year 2015 (filed 2016), sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000867773/000086777316000101/spwr_01032016x10-k.htm, fetched 2026-09-27, which states approximately 10 percent to 15 percent of United States dealers had earned Elite status and approximately 25 percent to 35 percent had earned Premier status (that is a distribution of dealer count by tier, not a commission rate).

**Basis of pay.** SunPower sells product to dealers (a manufacturer-to-dealer wholesale relationship); dealers install, maintain and service, earning their own retail-installed-price margin, similar in shape to the HVAC OEM dealer model above. No public per-watt commission paid by SunPower to dealers was found; dealers are a sales channel for SunPower hardware, not commissioned salespeople for a SunPower-owned project.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** As above (10-K FY2015, fetched 2026-09-27). No margin percentage disclosed.

**Everything else.** Not public. SunPower's dealer network model appears to be fee/margin based like the HVAC OEM channel, not a profit-share model, but this is an inference from the tier structure, not a direct disclosure of dealer economics.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** SunPower's residential/dealer business was largely divested and restructured (Blue Raven/Complete Solar, Sunder Energy installation agreement referenced in a 2024 SunPower investor release, investors.sunpower.com/news-releases/news-release-details/complete-solars-blue-raven-signs-installation-agreement-sunder, fetched 2026-09-27) amid SunPower's broader financial distress; this is relevant "how it plays out" context (a channel-dependent residential solar business model under severe stress) even though it does not give a specific commission number.

### 8b. Sunrun

**Who the partner is.** Sunrun both employs direct sales reps and sells through third-party dealers/installers; it also has been a defendant alongside named dealer partners.

**Basis of pay to direct sales reps.** Job postings describe "uncapped commission"; Sunrun's own recruiting material states 50 percent of first-year Sales Specialists earned 100,000 dollars or more in 2024, with the top 10 percent of new hires averaging 205,020 dollars (source: aggregated from Sunrun job postings via themuse.com, fetched 2026-09-27; this is Sunrun's own recruiting claim, not an independently audited figure, and should be labeled as company-reported). Industry-wide, solar sales commissions are generally structured as cents per watt or dollars per kW of installed nameplate capacity (general industry description, not Sunrun-specific rate, fetched 2026-09-27); no specific Sunrun per-watt commission rate was found publicly.

**Rates and tiers.** Not public for the per-watt rate itself.

**Stacking, attribution, duration and tail, payment timing, clawbacks.** Not public.

**Who bears performance risk.** This is where Sunrun's third-party dealer model has generated real legal exposure, which is directly relevant to Energy Plus's "who bears performance risk" question. The Connecticut Attorney General sued Sunrun along with two of its dealer/installer partners, Bright Planet Solar Inc. and Elevate Solar Solutions LLC, on 2024-07-19 (portal.ct.gov/ag/press-releases/2024-press-releases/attorney-general-tong-sues-sunrun, fetched 2026-09-27), alleging the dealers impersonated consumers to execute contracts, forged signatures on lease agreements, installed non-functional systems, failed to obtain permits, and locked homeowners into long-term contracts (one example cited: 2.9 percent annual escalators totaling 135,693 dollars in undisclosed lifetime charges) without informed consent. The complaint holds Sunrun jointly accountable for its dealer network's conduct, which is the clearest evidence in this whole research stream that a principal (Sunrun) cannot fully outsource performance and conduct risk to a commissioned dealer channel; regulators will come after the platform, not just the dealer.

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay.** Not public.

**Transparency.** Low; the CT AG complaint specifically alleges lack of informed consent and undisclosed escalating costs to the end customer, evidence of low transparency in that channel's sales practice, not necessarily in Sunrun's internal partner-pay documentation (which remains non-public either way).

**Evidence of how it plays out.** Beyond the Connecticut case: a 2019 New Jersey settlement involving Vivint Solar (later a Sunrun subsidiary as of the October 2020 acquisition) for 122,000 dollars over sales-practice issues predates the Sunrun merger; Sunrun separately agreed to pay up to 5.5 million dollars in 2024 to settle a TCPA class action over illegal marketing robocalls (topclassactions.com/lawsuit-settlements/tcpa/company-will-settle-sunrun-lawsuit-over-robocalling-for-5-5m/, fetched 2026-09-27). Trade press (time.com/7008754/federal-government-solar-industry-scams/, fetched 2026-09-27) describes an active federal government review of solar industry sales practices as of mid-2026, naming Sunrun among companies under scrutiny, though as of this research date no formal FTC enforcement order specific to Sunrun's dealer-commission practices was confirmed; an earlier claim circulating that the FTC had already reached a 98 million dollar settlement with Sunrun could not be verified in this pass and should not be treated as fact. Treat "FTC review ongoing" as the accurate, sourced statement, and treat any specific settlement dollar figure beyond the New Jersey and TCPA settlements above as unconfirmed.

**Verdict for SunPower and Sunrun together.** The residential solar dealer/commission channel is the highest-risk analog in this whole research stream for exactly the failure mode Dan is worried about with the "bring in a partner" fee: commissioned, volume-driven, multi-party sales channels with weak oversight produce deceptive sales practices, and regulators hold the platform (not just the dealer) liable. This is a strong argument for keeping Energy Plus's one-closer rule, requiring real work for real pay, and being conservative about any override/recruiting layer that could incentivize a partner to "just get the deal signed" without protecting the client.

## 9. Generac PowerPro

**Who the partner is.** Generac's dealer network, with named tiers including PowerPro, Direct, Select, Elite, Elite+, Premiere and Prestige (support.generac.com, fetched 2026-09-27); PowerPro Elite is described as Generac's highest elective standard for training, technical skill and proven customer satisfaction.

**Basis of pay.** Same margin/rebate structure as the HVAC OEM channel: dealers buy at a tiered wholesale discount off Minimum Advertised Price (MAP) and sell at retail. Trade-press aggregation (multiple sources fetched 2026-09-27, treat as estimates, not Generac's own published rate card) describes dealer pricing as roughly 5 percent (Select tier) to 11.6 percent (Premier-equivalent tier) below MAP plus shipping, on top of which Generac pays a further rebate accrual. A 2026 contractor-business trade blog (pipelineon.com/blog/electrician-generator-install/, fetched 2026-09-27) states PowerPro Elite dealers receive 30 to 60 routed leads per quarter from generac.com and the dealer locator, plus a 4 percent to 8 percent rebate accrual on equipment purchases, contingent on a minimum install volume of roughly 15 to 25 units per year depending on territory. A business-valuation guide aimed at buyers of generator-service companies (ctacquisitions.com/guides/generator-service-business-valuation/, fetched 2026-09-27) independently corroborates that Premier/Elite OEM dealer status is "the single most heavily weighted screening factor" in an acquisition, worth a 1x to 2x EBITDA valuation premium, and that a Premier dealer in a hurricane-exposed metro can get 200 to 600 qualified leads per month at zero customer-acquisition cost, which is a different (larger) lead-volume claim than the pipelineon figure and should be treated as directional trade estimate, not a reconciled fact; the two sources disagree on scale, which is itself worth noting.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** As above; both figures are trade-press or industry-blog estimates (pipelineon.com and ctacquisitions.com, both fetched 2026-09-27), not Generac's own published dealer agreement, which is not public.

**What is a fee vs a share.** Entirely fee/margin/rebate based, same as the HVAC OEM channel; no profit-share layer.

**Stacking rules.** Dealers can stack the wholesale discount, the rebate accrual, and routed leads simultaneously; this is the norm in this channel, same as HVAC.

**Attribution, duration and tail.** Not applicable/not public; tier status must be earned and maintained against volume and training requirements, not a one-time achievement.

**Payment timing, holdbacks, clawbacks.** Not public.

**Who bears performance risk.** The dealer bears installation and service risk; Generac's product warranty covers the equipment itself.

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay.** None found; single-level dealer-to-manufacturer relationship only.

**Transparency.** Tier names and general benefit descriptions are public; actual discount percentages and rebate accrual rates are not published by Generac itself and had to be triangulated from trade sources, which disagree with each other on lead-volume scale, so confidence is moderate, not high.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** A Mike Holt electrical-trade forum thread on Generac dealer pricing exists (forums.mikeholt.com, referenced via search but returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch, 2026-09-27) and could not be independently reviewed in this pass; flag as a lead for follow-up, not a confirmed source.

**Verdict.** Generac PowerPro is another clean confirmation of the equipment-dealer norm: margin plus rebate plus lead-routing, no profit share, no recruiting override, heavy stacking. It reinforces the same conclusion as the HVAC OEM section.

## 10. Demand response and grid services channel partners: Voltus, Enel X, CPower, Leap

This category is structurally the closest analog in the entire research plan to Energy Plus's savings-funded, performance-based model, because DR aggregators are themselves paid by grid operators based on delivered performance, and they in turn share that revenue with the asset owner (the "customer") and, in some cases, a channel partner who brought the site.

### 10a. Voltus

**Who the partner is.** Voltus explicitly runs a channel-partner program (voltus.co/channel-partners, fetched 2026-09-27) naming partners including Scale Microgrid Solutions, Enertiv, Vurge, Gravity, Branch Energy, Vistra, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries and PowerSecure. Partners can either let Voltus sell demand response directly to the partner's end customers, or the partner's own sales team can sell it with Voltus support.

**Basis of pay.** Voltus states it offers three partnership financial structures on that page: revenue share, fixed price, and upfront payments, without publishing the percentages or dollar amounts for any of the three (voltus.co/channel-partners, fetched 2026-09-27, direct fetch confirms no percentages disclosed). Voltus states its goal is to give "customers transparency into their earnings" from every kW monetized, but that transparency claim is about the underlying DR revenue itself, not about the channel-partner commission structure, which is not published.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** Not public; terms are individually negotiated ("Speak to an energy expert" call to action).

**What is a fee vs a share.** Voltus offers both explicitly as named options (fixed price = fee; revenue share = share; upfront payment = a third category, essentially a signing bonus or capitalized fee), which is itself a useful structural idea: naming multiple payment-basis options rather than forcing one model on every partner type.

**Stacking rules, attribution, duration and tail, payment timing, clawbacks.** Not public.

**Who bears performance risk.** Not stated for the partner relationship; industry-wide, the aggregator (Voltus) bears settlement and dispatch performance risk with the grid operator/utility, and passes a share of the resulting revenue down to the site owner and/or channel partner.

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay.** Not public.

**Transparency.** Public and reasonably detailed on the existence of three distinct payment models; not transparent on the actual rates.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** Voltus is independently ranked the number 1 aggregator by gigawatts under management in Wood Mackenzie's 2025 Virtual Power Plant report (voltus.co/press/voltus-wood-mackenzie-2025-vpp-report, fetched 2026-09-27), suggesting its channel and direct-sales model scales effectively, though this says nothing directly about partner-pay fairness.

### 10b. Enel X (Enel North America)

**Who the partner is.** Enel X is one of the largest demand response aggregators and VPP operators in North America and also functions as an exclusive DR program manager for utilities in several states (enelnorthamerica.com, fetched 2026-09-27).

**Basis of pay.** Enel North America publishes a "benefit share" model for its financed energy solutions (solar/battery projects), illustrated with a 50:50 split example: if a system is projected to deliver 5 million dollars in cumulative benefits over 15 years, Enel and the customer split it 50:50, and that ratio stays fixed even if actual realized benefits move up or down with market conditions (enelnorthamerica.com/insights/blogs/energy-solutions-financing-benefit-share, fetched 2026-09-27, directly confirmed by fetch). Important caveat: this is the customer-facing financing benefit-share model, not a confirmed channel-partner commission rate; there is a meaningful difference between "what Enel splits with the site owner" and "what Enel pays a partner who brought the deal," and this research could not find the latter publicly disclosed.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** 50:50 split example as above, dated via the Enel North America blog post, fetched 2026-09-27 (no separate publish date visible on the page itself).

**What is a fee vs a share.** A share, expressed as a percentage of cumulative dollar benefit over a fixed multi-year term, with the ratio held constant regardless of how actual results track versus projection, which is a clean, simple mechanic worth studying for its "fixed ratio, floating dollar amount" design.

**Stacking, attribution.** Not public for a channel-partner scenario specifically.

**Duration and tail.** The example given runs the full 15-year projected benefit horizon at a constant ratio, i.e., the split does not step down over time; this is a genuine design choice worth comparing against Energy Plus's "partner keeps earning on phase two" principle, since Enel's model bakes the long tail into a single multi-year term rather than treating renewals as new deals.

**Payment timing, holdbacks, clawbacks.** Not public.

**Who bears performance risk.** The framing explicitly protects the customer from downside: the ratio is fixed even if benefits come in at 3 to 6.5 million instead of the 5 million projected in the illustration, meaning both parties share the up and down proportionally rather than the customer taking the shortfall first; this is a different risk allocation than Energy Plus's "who absorbs a clawback" question and worth flagging in the synthesis as a genuine alternative philosophy (proportional share of variance versus fixed floor plus shared upside).

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay, transparency.** Not public for channel partners specifically.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** Enel X is named as one of the largest DR aggregators/VPP operators across multiple industry vendor-guide sources (codibly.com/blog/articles/demand-response-companies, fetched 2026-09-27) and recently (announced December 2025) partnered with Leap to expand utility DR access (enelnorthamerica.com/about-us/newsroom/search-press/press/2025/12/leap-vpps, and leap.energy/news/leap-and-enel-partner-to-accelerate-nationwide-growth-of-virtual-power-plants, both fetched 2026-09-27), which is itself a live example of two DR platforms partnering with each other rather than one recruiting the other's customers away, an instructive "how it plays out" data point for platform-to-platform cooperation versus competition.

### 10c. CPower

**Who the partner is.** CPower is a demand response and energy-flexibility aggregator serving commercial and industrial customers and also positions itself as working with channel partners/brokers, though the specific partner-facing page was not independently located in this pass (only the customer-facing FAQ was reviewed).

**Basis of pay.** CPower's own FAQ (cpowerenergy.com/demand-response-faq/, fetched 2026-09-27, direct fetch) describes a dual-component payment structure to the participant: a guaranteed fixed payment per kW of enrolled capacity, plus a performance-based reward tied to the amount of energy reduction actually delivered during a test or grid event. No percentage revenue share, and no specifics on how performance risk is allocated (i.e., what happens if a participant fails to curtail), are disclosed in that document.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** Not public.

**What is a fee vs a share.** A hybrid: the guaranteed per-kW payment functions like a capacity fee, and the event-performance reward functions like a share of realized value; CPower does not describe this as a percentage of gross ISO payments the way industry-wide vendor guides describe aggregator economics generally (see 10d below).

**Stacking, attribution, duration and tail, payment timing.** Not public.

**Clawbacks.** Not stated in the FAQ; typical for the DR industry generally (not CPower-specifically confirmed) that non-performance during a called event reduces or eliminates that event's performance payment, and repeated non-performance can risk removal from a program, but this is a general industry inference, not a CPower-specific disclosure.

**Who bears performance risk.** Not explicitly allocated in the FAQ; the guaranteed-payment component suggests CPower absorbs some baseline risk, while the performance component passes delivery risk back to the participant.

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay, transparency.** Not public. CPower does state it has paid out over 1.2 billion dollars in demand response and energy flexibility payments to customers since 2015 (cpowerenergy.com/demand-response-faq/, fetched 2026-09-27), which is a scale claim, not a rate disclosure.

### 10d. Leap

**Who the partner is.** Leap is a software-only, white-label VPP/DR enablement platform; its "partners" are technology providers (battery, EV charging, smart building companies) and installers/OEMs whose devices Leap enrolls into grid programs under the partner's own brand, not traditional referral brokers. Leap states it empowers more than 100 technology partners and manages over 400,000 energy sites and devices (leap.energy, fetched 2026-09-27).

**Basis of pay.** Leap markets a per-kW-of-nominated-capacity revenue model to its technology partners, stating that "integrated partners earn 87 percent more revenue per kW of nominated capacity by performing better and stacking more programs" than non-integrated approaches (leap.energy marketing material, referenced via WebSearch, fetched 2026-09-27; the 87 percent figure is Leap's own marketing claim and was not independently verified against a specific case study in this pass, so it should be treated as a company-reported, not independently audited, figure). This confirms per-kW/per-unit as the basis, and confirms that Leap explicitly encourages "stacking more programs" as a revenue lever, i.e., stacking is a designed-in feature of the DR/VPP category generally, not an edge case.

**Rates and tiers, with source and date.** No specific dollar-per-kW rate published; the 87 percent relative-improvement claim is the only quantified figure found, sourced to Leap's own marketing (fetched 2026-09-27).

**What is a fee vs a share.** Per-kW/per-unit revenue, closer to a unit-price fee for enrolled capacity than a percentage-of-profit share, though it functions economically like a share of the value Leap itself realizes from grid programs.

**Stacking rules.** Explicitly encouraged; Leap's own value proposition depends on partners stacking multiple grid programs on the same enrolled capacity.

**Attribution, duration, payment timing, clawbacks.** Not public.

**Who bears performance risk.** Not explicitly stated; the white-label model suggests Leap's platform handles enrollment, bidding, dispatch and settlement (per the general aggregator description in 10d below) while the underlying device performance (does the battery/EV charger actually curtail) risk sits with the technology partner and end site.

**Recruiting or sub-partner pay.** None found; this is a two-party platform-to-technology-partner model, not a multi-level recruiting structure.

**Transparency.** Leap markets an "Incentives Gateway" (incentives.leap.energy, fetched 2026-09-27) intended to help partners find and stack applicable utility/grid incentive programs, which is itself a transparency tool, though it is about program availability, not Leap's own commission math.

**Evidence of how it plays out.** The December 2025 Leap-Enel partnership (leap.energy/news/leap-and-enel-partner-to-accelerate-nationwide-growth-of-virtual-power-plants, fetched 2026-09-27) is explicitly framed as adding "valuable revenue for Leap's C&I partner base" by connecting Leap's existing technology-partner base to Enel-managed utility DR programs in Washington, Arizona and the Tennessee Valley Authority territory, expanding over time; this is a real example of a platform bringing its channel partners a second monetization avenue rather than one platform poaching the other's relationships.

### General DR/aggregator revenue-share benchmark (applies across Voltus, Enel X, CPower, Leap)

Multiple industry vendor-comparison sources describe curtailment aggregator economics generally (not attributed to one specific company's contract) as: "the aggregator handles enrollment, market bidding, dispatch, settlement and customer service in exchange for a revenue share that typically lands between 60 percent and 80 percent of the gross payment" from the ISO/grid operator, meaning the underlying site/customer typically keeps 20 percent to 40 percent of the gross wholesale-market payment. Source: codibly.com/blog/articles/demand-response-companies, fetched 2026-09-27; the article itself cites no external source or regulatory filing for this range, so it should be treated as an industry-observer estimate, not a documented company disclosure, and applied as a general benchmark across the category rather than attributed to any one named company in this list.

**Verdict for the DR group as a whole.** This category is the best fit in the whole research plan for the "who bears performance risk and what's the clawback" half of Dan's question, because DR aggregators are paid on actual delivered performance by a grid operator and pass a share (60 percent to 80 percent aggregator, 20 percent to 40 percent site owner, industry-wide estimate) or a hybrid guaranteed-plus-performance payment (CPower's model) down the chain; non-performance risk sits, in practice, with whoever controls the physical curtailment (the site/technology partner), with the aggregator absorbing settlement and enrollment risk. Leap's explicit "stack more programs, earn more revenue" pitch and Enel X's fixed-ratio, variable-dollar benefit share are the two most exportable ideas: (1) design the fee/share math so stacking is safe and even encouraged where it doesn't double-pay the same contribution, and (2) fix the split ratio for the life of a multi-year engagement rather than renegotiating it deal by deal.

- Carrier, Lennox, Generac: actual dealer margin and rebate percentages are not published by the manufacturers; figures used here are trade-press or industry-blog estimates, clearly labeled, and in Generac's case two trade sources disagree with each other on lead-volume scale.
- Voltus, CPower, Leap: none publish an actual channel-partner or revenue-share percentage; only the payment basis and, in Leap's case, a self-reported relative-improvement claim, are public.
- The widely circulated claim that Sunrun paid a 98 million dollar FTC settlement over dealer sales practices could not be verified in this research pass and is not included as fact; the confirmed regulatory actions are the Connecticut AG suit (2024-07-19), a New Jersey Vivint Solar settlement predating the Sunrun merger, and a TCPA class-action settlement, plus a general, ongoing federal review of solar sales practices reported as of mid-2026.
